# Sub-Assembly — Body-in-White, Powertrain, and Chassis Preparation

Sub-assembly is the manufacturing phase in which the major structural and mechanical systems of a passenger vehicle are built as discrete units before they converge on the main assembly line. The most significant of these is the body-in-white — the bare welded steel or aluminium shell of the vehicle before any paint, trim, glass, or mechanical components are added. Alongside body-in-white construction, dedicated sub-assembly stations prepare the powertrain, the chassis cradle, and major interior subassemblies in parallel, so that when the main line clock ticks, each unit is ready to mate.

## Body-in-White Construction

Body-in-white (BIW) fabrication is the backbone of the sub-assembly stage. Stamped steel panels — roof, floor pan, front and rear rails, A-pillars, B-pillars, C-pillars, door apertures, and body sides — arrive at the body shop either from an on-site stamping press facility or from a first-tier supplier. Robotic weld cells join these panels using a combination of resistance spot welding (RSW), metal inert gas (MIG) welding, and structural adhesives at bonded joints. A typical passenger car body-in-white involves 3,000–6,000 individual weld points.

Geometry fixtures — large, precision-machined steel jigs — hold the panels in their exact design positions during welding to ensure dimensional accuracy. A body that exits geometry at the correct tolerance is the prerequisite for everything that follows: door gaps, panel flushness, windshield seal fit, and roof rail alignment are all locked in at the body-in-white stage and cannot be meaningfully corrected afterwards without major rework.

**Dimensional inspection.** After the body shell exits the primary weld cell, it passes through a co-ordinate measuring machine (CMM) station or a laser scanning gate. Hundreds of measurement points on the body are checked against nominal design tolerances, typically ±0.5 mm on critical apertures. Bodies outside tolerance are either routed to a rework station or scrapped, depending on the nature and magnitude of the deviation.

## Powertrain Sub-Assembly

For electric vehicles, powertrain sub-assembly centres on the high-voltage battery pack and the electric drive unit. Battery cell modules — pre-assembled groups of prismatic, cylindrical, or pouch cells — are loaded into the battery pack housing, connected in series-parallel configurations, and sealed. The Battery Management System (BMS) PCB, cooling plate manifold, high-voltage busbars, and service disconnect are all installed in the pack sub-assembly area under controlled temperature and humidity conditions. The completed pack is then leak-tested and given a brief electrical verification cycle before it is cleared for main-line fitment.

For internal combustion and hybrid powertrains, engine and transmission sub-assembly follows a similar parallel-station logic: cylinder heads, valve trains, crankshafts, and ancillary drives are built up at dedicated benches, and the completed short or long block is passed to a run-in test rig before joining the main line queue.

## Chassis and Suspension Preparation

Front and rear sub-frames, steering columns, braking system components, and wheel-hub assemblies are often pre-assembled into modular "corner modules" that can be lowered onto the body as a unit on the main line. This modular approach reduces main-line dwell time and allows complex suspension geometry settings to be pre-set and verified before installation.

## Quality Checkpoints During Sub-Assembly

Sub-assembly stations incorporate inline quality checks before units are cleared to proceed:

- **Body-in-white geometry check** — CMM or laser gate confirms weld quality and panel alignment.
- **Torque verification** — critical fasteners are tightened with calibrated electronic torque tools and settings are logged per VIN.
- **Battery pack leak and electrical check** — confirms cell balance, BMS communication, and seal integrity before the pack leaves the sub-assembly area.
- **Weld quality sampling** — periodic destructive testing of weld coupons from control bodies confirms the weld schedule remains within specification.

## Typical Duration

Body-in-white construction for a passenger car body shell takes four to eight hours of active process time across multiple robotic cells, though the vehicle may queue between stations. Battery pack sub-assembly for a large-format EV pack runs six to twelve hours. Because these activities run in parallel with other vehicles in the queue, the elapsed calendar time from the start of a customer's vehicle's sub-assembly to completion is typically one to two production shifts.
