# Shipped — Outbound Logistics from Plant to Port and Dealer

When a vehicle is shipped from the factory, it enters the outbound logistics network — a multi-modal supply chain that moves finished vehicles from the completed vehicle compound at the plant to distribution centres, ports, rail ramps, and ultimately to the dealer's physical lot. The shipped stage spans from the moment the vehicle drives off the end-of-line test area and is confirmed as cleared-for-shipment, to the moment it arrives at the destination dealer or distribution point. For domestic deliveries this may be a matter of days; for export vehicles it can span three to eight weeks or more.

## The Finished Vehicle Compound

After clearing end-of-line testing, a vehicle is moved to the plant's finished vehicle compound (FVC) — a large open-air or covered storage area, typically holding several hundred to several thousand vehicles at any point. In the FVC the vehicle receives its protective transport measures: door and wing mirror caps, seat covers, paint protection film on high-abrasion areas such as the bonnet leading edge and door sills, and in some programmes a full-body breathable cover for vehicles with special paint finishes.

The vehicle's position in the FVC is tracked by the plant's vehicle movement system (VMS), which records the parking bay and enables the logistics team to call specific vehicles for loading without a physical search of the compound.

## Rail and Road Transport

Most passenger car plants are served by dedicated automotive rail facilities that accommodate rail car carriers — purpose-built wagons that carry four to ten passenger vehicles on two or three decks. Rail is the preferred mode for long-haul domestic transport due to its lower cost per vehicle and reduced risk of road damage over distance.

For shorter distances to regional distribution centres or directly to dealers within the plant's primary market, road transport using car transporter trucks (auto carriers) is common. A standard car transporter carries six to nine passenger vehicles depending on size and stacking configuration.

When vehicles are loaded onto car carriers or rail wagons, a condition report is completed for each vehicle noting any pre-existing marks or damage. This condition report is the baseline against which any transport damage claims are assessed at delivery.

## Vessel Shipping for Export Markets

Vehicles destined for export markets are transported to a port vehicle processing centre (VPC) by road or rail. At the VPC, each vehicle is prepared for ocean transit: this involves a more thorough cleaning, additional paint protection film or wax application, draining of fluids to transport-safe levels if required, and any market-specific pre-delivery options that are fitted at port (roof rails, market-specific accessories, regulatory label sets).

Ocean transport of passenger vehicles uses roll-on, roll-off (RoRo) vessels — purpose-designed ships on which vehicles are driven aboard via stern or side ramps and secured on multiple vehicle decks. RoRo ships carry anywhere from a few hundred to several thousand vehicles per voyage and operate on scheduled trade-lane services between major automotive export hubs.

Transit times on major automotive trade lanes range from ten days (Europe to the US East Coast) to twenty-five days (Europe or the US to Asia Pacific and Australia), depending on the route and vessel schedule. The vehicle's shipment status is updated when the vessel departs port (departure manifest confirmed) and again when the vessel arrives at the destination port.

## Vehicle Tracking During Transit

Modern outbound logistics platforms provide real-time or near-real-time vehicle tracking through the shipment chain. The vehicle's VIN is scanned at each handoff — plant load, rail interchange, port arrival, vessel load, vessel discharge, road carrier load, and dealer delivery. These scan events feed the manufacturer's track-and-trace system and, where integrated with a connected vehicle app, provide the customer with visible progress through the shipment phase.

The most common question customers ask during the shipped stage is why the tracker has not moved in several days. In most cases this reflects the vehicle waiting in the FVC for loading onto the next available carrier, or in transit on a RoRo vessel where intermediate status updates are unavailable. Delays in the shipped stage are rarely attributable to the individual vehicle — they reflect carrier availability and route schedule.

## Pre-Delivery Inspection Handoff

The vehicle's condition at delivery to the dealer is documented by a receiving inspection that compares the condition report from the plant against the vehicle's current condition. Any transport damage — stone chips, scuffs from car carrier chains or straps, windshield chips from road debris — is noted and the vehicle is either routed for rectification before customer delivery, or the claim is lodged with the carrier or shipping company.
