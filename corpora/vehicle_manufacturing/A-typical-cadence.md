# Typical Build Cadence — How Long Does It Take to Build a Car?

One of the most common questions from customers who have placed an order is "how long does it actually take to build a car?" The honest answer is that the build time — the period of active manufacturing — is shorter than most customers expect, but the calendar time from order to delivery is considerably longer. Understanding the difference between active build time and total order-to-delivery time helps customers read their progress updates with realistic expectations.

## Active Manufacturing Time

The active manufacturing time for a passenger vehicle — the total hours of direct process work invested in the vehicle from the start of body-in-white stamping and welding to the end of end-of-line testing — typically spans fifteen to thirty hours of process time for a volume passenger car.

Breaking this down by stage:

| Stage | Typical active process time |
|---|---|
| Body-in-white (stamping + welding) | 4–8 hours |
| Paint shop (pre-treatment through top-coat cure + inspection) | 8–16 hours |
| Sub-assembly (powertrain + chassis — runs in parallel) | 4–12 hours |
| Final assembly (trim + marriage point + fluid fill) | 8–20 hours |
| Quality control + end-of-line testing | 2–4 hours |
| **Total active process time** | **15–30 hours** |

These stages do not run sequentially for a single vehicle. The vehicle's body-in-white is in the paint shop while a later customer's order is in body welding, and the sub-assembly of the powertrain runs concurrently with the paint shop cycle. The vehicle moves from stage to stage as each is completed, waiting in queue between stages when the downstream station is occupied with another vehicle.

## Factory Throughput vs. Individual Build Time

A vehicle assembly plant producing 200,000–400,000 vehicles per year runs at a takt time of 60–90 seconds between vehicles leaving the end of the line. At 60 seconds per vehicle, the line produces 1 vehicle per minute, or approximately 480 vehicles per shift. But this does not mean any individual vehicle takes 60 seconds to build — it means a new vehicle completes every 60 seconds. The individual vehicle has been on the line for the entire duration, advancing one station per takt cycle.

The time any specific customer's vehicle spends in the factory — from the moment the body shop begins welding its body-in-white to the moment it rolls off end-of-line testing — is typically two to five production shifts (16–40 hours of clock time), because the vehicle queues between stages and each stage processes it through a sequence of stations.

## Calendar Time from Order to Delivery

The calendar time from order placement to vehicle delivery to the customer is a different, much larger number. It includes:

- **Order processing and payment confirmation**: 0–2 weeks
- **Production scheduling cycle (waiting for a build slot)**: 1–8 weeks depending on model demand and order bank depth
- **Time in production slot queue before body shop start**: 1–4 weeks
- **Active manufacturing at the plant**: 1–2 weeks of calendar time (including queue time between stages)
- **Finished vehicle compound and plant departure**: 1–3 days
- **Domestic transport by road or rail**: 1–7 days
- **Export ocean shipping (if applicable)**: 10–25 days
- **Port vehicle processing centre and importation**: 3–10 days
- **Dealer pre-delivery inspection and preparation**: 2–5 days

For a domestic order of a volume model with average demand, the total calendar time from order to delivery is typically eight to sixteen weeks. For a high-demand model, an export vehicle, or an order placed at the start of a new model year when order banks are deep, twenty-four weeks or more is common. Limited-production and bespoke models may have order-to-delivery times of six months to over a year.

## Why Lead Times Vary So Much

The dominant variable in lead time is not manufacturing speed — plants are highly optimised and the active build time is relatively stable. The dominant variable is the depth of the order bank relative to weekly production capacity. A plant producing 3,000 vehicles per week with a 30,000-vehicle order bank has a ten-week order bank; the same plant with a 15,000-vehicle order bank has a five-week order bank. When a new model launches and generates strong demand, order banks fill quickly and lead times extend. As demand normalises, lead times shorten.

Supply chain disruptions — component shortages, port congestion, carrier capacity — can inject additional delays at any point in the order-to-delivery sequence, and these are typically communicated by the manufacturer or dealer with revised estimated delivery windows.
