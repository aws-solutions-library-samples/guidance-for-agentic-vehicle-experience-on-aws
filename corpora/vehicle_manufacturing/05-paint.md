# Paint Stage — Automotive Paint Shop, Cure Temperatures, and Surface Quality

The paint stage is one of the most capital-intensive and technically demanding phases of passenger vehicle manufacturing. An automotive paint shop is a self-contained manufacturing facility within the plant, typically occupying 20–30% of the total factory floor area. The body-in-white shell, after completing the sub-assembly body shop, enters the paint shop as bare metal and exits as a fully painted and cured body, ready for trim and mechanical installation on the main assembly line. The entire paint-shop sequence for a passenger car body typically runs eight to sixteen hours of active process time.

## The Automotive Paint Shop Sequence

### Pre-Treatment and Phosphate Coating

The body-in-white is conveyed through a multi-stage pre-treatment tunnel before any paint is applied. Pre-treatment removes mill scale, metal oxides, drawing lubricants, and handling soils accumulated during stamping and welding. The sequence involves alkaline degreasing baths, rinse stages, and a phosphate conversion coating step in which the metal surface is chemically converted to a crystalline zinc or iron phosphate layer. The phosphate layer serves two functions: it dramatically improves paint adhesion by providing a textured key for subsequent coatings, and it provides a first line of corrosion protection on raw metal surfaces inside box sections and cavities that later coatings cannot easily penetrate.

### Electrodeposition Primer (E-Coat)

After phosphate pre-treatment, the body is immersed in a large electro-deposition bath containing an aqueous paint emulsion. An electrical potential is applied between the bath and the body, causing the paint to migrate to and deposit uniformly on all metal surfaces — including internal box sections, door inner panels, and weld seams that spray application cannot reliably reach. This e-coat or cathodic dip primer layer, typically 15–25 micrometers thick, is the primary corrosion barrier for the vehicle's body structure and is designed to provide protection for ten or more years under normal operating conditions. The coated body passes through a dedicated e-coat oven, curing at approximately 175–185°C for 20–30 minutes.

### Sealer and Underbody Coating

After e-coat curing, seam sealers — two-component polyurethane or hot-melt materials — are applied by robot or by hand to all weld seams, hemmed flanges, and any joints where water ingress is a risk. Stone-chip and underbody protective coatings are simultaneously applied to the floor pan, wheel arches, and lower body sides. These materials are heavier-bodied than the surface coatings above them and are specifically formulated to resist the abrasion of road debris and gravel at motorway speeds throughout the vehicle's service life.

### Primer Surfacer

A primer surfacer is applied by electrostatic spray robots over the full exterior of the body to fill minor surface irregularities from the stamped panels and welds and to provide a smooth, consistent substrate for the colour coat. Primer surfacer is applied wet at 30–40 micrometers and cured at 155–165°C for 20–25 minutes. At this stage, a visual and tactile inspection identifies any surface defects in the primer layer — runs, sags, contamination, or unacceptable texture — which are sanded and re-applied before the body advances to the colour coat booths.

### Base Coat (Colour Coat)

The base coat carries the customer's chosen colour. Modern automotive paint shops use electrostatic rotary atomiser robots ("bell robots") that apply paint at very high transfer efficiency to minimise material waste and overspray. Solid colours are applied in one or two passes; metallic and pearlescent colours require two to four thin passes to orient the metallic aluminium flake or pearl pigment particles consistently. Consistent flake orientation is critical to achieving the "depth" and colour-shift effects that distinguish a premium metallic finish from an entry-level one.

Base coat is a thin, high-colour-saturation layer — typically 12–20 micrometers dry film thickness — and is not cured as a standalone step in the three-stage wet-on-wet process now standard in most automotive paint shops.

### Clear Coat

The clear coat lacquer is applied over the wet base coat (wet-on-wet process), before the base coat is cured. This approach — applying two wet layers before the single combined cure — reduces energy consumption and takt time. Clear coat is typically 35–50 micrometers dry film thickness and is formulated for UV resistance, scratch resistance, and gloss depth. The combined base coat and clear coat layers are cured in a top-coat oven at 140–145°C for 25–30 minutes. Temperature uniformity across the oven's cross-section is tightly controlled: a deviation of more than ±5°C can produce visible differences in gloss level or cause solvent entrapment defects in the paint film.

## Quality Inspection in the Paint Shop

After the top-coat cure, every body passes through a dedicated paint inspection station. Inspection is conducted under high-intensity fluorescent or LED raked lighting — the "gloss booth" or "orange peel booth" — which reveals surface defects invisible under normal ambient lighting. Automated optical inspection systems increasingly supplement or replace manual inspection on high-volume lines, scanning the body surface at micrometer resolution for contamination (embedded particles), runs (excess material that pooled before curing), orange peel (insufficient levelling of the wet film surface), and solvent pops (microscopic craters from solvent vapour escaping through a partially cured surface skin).

Bodies with defects within the defined rework tolerance are directed to a finishing station where minor defects are polished or filled. Bodies with defects beyond the rework threshold are returned to the paint shop for strip and repaint — a costly intervention that plant quality teams track closely as a key performance indicator.

## Cure Temperature Summary

| Layer | Cure temperature | Cure duration |
|---|---|---|
| E-coat primer | 175–185°C | 20–30 min |
| Primer surfacer | 155–165°C | 20–25 min |
| Base coat + clear coat (combined) | 140–145°C | 25–30 min |
