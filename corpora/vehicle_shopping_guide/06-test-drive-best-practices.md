# Test Drive Best Practices — What to Feel For, What to Check, and How to Use the Drive to Make a Decision

A test drive is the single most powerful piece of research you can do before buying a vehicle. It is also, in most showroom interactions, conducted far too briefly and far too close to the dealership to reveal anything meaningful. This guide explains how to arrange and conduct a test drive that gives you genuinely useful information — and how to translate what you feel into a buying decision.

## Before the Test Drive: Prepare a Checklist

Arriving at the dealership without a plan means the salesperson controls the itinerary. Prepare before you arrive:

1. **Know which trim level you intend to test.** If you are evaluating a mid-trim vehicle, do not test drive a top-trim demonstrator and then buy mid-trim expecting the same experience. Confirm with the dealership that the demonstrator matches the trim level you plan to purchase.
2. **Bring your regular passenger load.** If you routinely drive with two rear-seat adults, bring them. The car that feels spacious empty can feel different with people in the back. If you normally carry a child seat, bring it and install it — confirm the ISOFIX anchors work with your seat and that the installed seat does not obstruct the front occupant's movement.
3. **Bring an item you regularly carry.** If you frequently load a specific sports bag, a stroller, a golf bag, or a tool kit into the trunk, bring it. Test whether it fits with the cargo floor in place, with the underfloor storage accessed, and with the seats down.
4. **Plan a route that includes the road conditions you drive on.** If your daily commute involves a poorly surfaced urban road, find one near the dealership. If you drive on a highway regularly, ask for a route that includes a highway stretch. Test drives around a smooth city block near the showroom tell you nothing useful about ride comfort.

## What to Assess During the Test Drive

### Visibility

Sit in the driver's seat and assess sightlines before moving. Adjust the mirrors fully and note:

- **A-pillar obstruction**: thick A-pillars (the upright between the windshield and the front side windows) create blind spots at intersections. Check whether the pillar obscures a pedestrian at a crosswalk when you are angled to turn.
- **Rear and side visibility**: in an SUV or crossover, the C-pillar and rear quarter panel can be thick. Assess whether you need the reversing camera to maneuver confidently, or whether you can judge the rear of the vehicle comfortably without it.
- **Windshield rake**: heavily angled windshields look aerodynamically elegant but can catch solar glare at low sun angles. Note whether the sun visor covers the full glare zone at the angle that represents your morning or evening commute.

### Ride Quality and Suspension Behavior

On your test route, assess:

- **Low-speed ride over rough surfaces**: does the suspension absorb expansion joints and potholed pavement without transmitting sharp impacts to the seat? A suspension tuned for sporty response can feel excellent at speed but jarring on a potholed city road.
- **Body control in corners**: take a roundabout or a sweeping bend at moderate speed. Does the body roll feel controlled, or does the vehicle lean significantly? Excessive body roll on an SUV is a common source of longer-term driver fatigue.
- **High-speed stability**: at highway speed, the car should feel planted and stable without requiring constant small steering corrections (a condition sometimes called "wandering" that indicates alignment or tire issues).

### Steering Feel and Effort

Modern electric power steering varies considerably between models in the same segment. On the test drive, assess:

- **Straight-line tracking**: does the car go where you point it without drift?
- **Low-speed effort**: how much effort is required to maneuver in a parking lot? Light steering eases parking but can feel vague at speed.
- **Feedback from the road**: does the steering communicate information about road surface and grip, or is it isolated and numb? Your preference here is personal — some buyers prefer isolation; others value communication.

### Powertrain Character

For ICE vehicles: note smooth gear changes, any hesitation on initial acceleration from standstill, and how the engine pulls through the middle of the rev range at highway cruising speed. Aggressive, poorly-calibrated gearboxes produce frequent, unnecessary downshifts under mild demand.

For electric and hybrid powertrains: note the immediate torque response from rest, the quality of the one-pedal driving feel (how smoothly does regenerative braking decelerate the car when you lift off the accelerator?), and the quietness of the cabin at highway speeds when the combustion engine is inactive.

### Cabin Comfort and Ergonomics

During a 30-minute drive, assess:

- **Seat comfort**: check that the lumbar support (if adjustable) is effective for your back. Long drives reveal seat comfort issues that a 10-minute test drive conceals.
- **Steering wheel reach and height adjustment**: can you find a driving position where you see the full instrument cluster above the steering wheel rim, with your arms slightly bent (not locked out) when gripping the wheel?
- **Controls you use most often**: can you operate the climate controls, volume, and audio source without looking away from the road? Physical controls are generally quicker than touchscreen menus for these frequently-used functions.
- **Infotainment responsiveness**: if the model has a large touchscreen, operate it while stationary (not while driving). Check the response latency — a touchscreen that lags 0.5 seconds per touch is a long-term irritation.

### Noise, Vibration, and Harshness (NVH)

NVH is the collective term for noise, vibration, and harshness — the qualities that determine how refined or rough the vehicle feels. At highway speed:

- **Road noise**: tire noise transmitted into the cabin is the dominant noise source on most vehicles at speed. It varies dramatically between tire types; confirm the demonstrator is wearing the same tires as the production model you will receive.
- **Wind noise**: wind noise around the A-pillars and door mirrors at highway speed is a common NVH issue on SUVs and crossovers. Close your eyes and listen for 30 seconds at 70 mph (110 km/h).
- **Vibration**: any persistent vibration through the floor, steering wheel, or seat at any speed should be noted and queried with the dealer.

## After the Test Drive: Debrief While the Memory Is Fresh

Sit quietly in the dealer's parking lot for five minutes after the drive and make notes on your phone or a notepad. Answer these questions immediately:

1. Did the vehicle feel larger or smaller than expected once you were inside?
2. Was there anything that felt unexpectedly good or unexpectedly poor?
3. Could you live with the NVH level on a daily commute?
4. Did the seating position feel natural, or did you spend the drive adjusting for a compromise?
5. Did you feel confident in the vehicle's sightlines and parking maneuvers?

If a second test drive on a different day produces different impressions, trust the second — your first impression includes novelty effects that a second drive corrects.

## Test Driving a BEV for the First Time

If you are test-driving a battery-electric vehicle and have not driven one before, the experience is genuinely different from ICE. Allow time to:

- Adjust to the instant torque response at low speeds — many new BEV drivers find they need to lighten their throttle input for the first few minutes
- Evaluate the regenerative braking feel — a strong regenerative braking setting (sometimes labeled "one-pedal mode") produces significant deceleration when you lift off the accelerator, which takes several minutes to become natural
- Listen to the cabin sound characteristics — without engine noise, tire and wind noise are more prominent, which can make a well-insulated BEV cabin feel quieter overall, or reveal road noise that a noisy ICE engine was masking
