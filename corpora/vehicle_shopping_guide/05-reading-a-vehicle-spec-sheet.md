# Reading a Four-Wheeler Spec Sheet — Range, kWh, DC Fast-Charge Speed, Cargo Volume, Tow Rating, and Curb Weight

A manufacturer's specification sheet is a dense document that mixes genuinely useful numbers with aspirational headline figures calibrated for press releases rather than for daily driving decisions. This guide explains what each key metric actually means, how to interpret the headline number, and which real-world adjustments to apply before drawing conclusions.

## Range (Electric Vehicles and PHEVs)

For a battery-electric vehicle (BEV) or plug-in hybrid (PHEV), the range figure published on the specification sheet is derived from a standardized government test cycle. In most markets this is the WLTP (Worldwide Harmonized Light Vehicle Test Procedure) in Europe and Asia, or the EPA combined cycle in North America.

**What the headline says**: "490 km (305 miles) WLTP" or "310 miles EPA."

**What it means in practice**: real-world range is lower than the headline figure for most drivers and most conditions. The test cycle is conducted at moderate temperatures (20–23 °C / 68–73 °F), at speeds that include urban, suburban, and highway segments, and without aggressive climate control use. In practice:

- Highway driving at 120 km/h (75 mph) uses 25–35 percent more energy per mile than the test cycle average because aerodynamic drag increases quadratically with speed
- Cold weather (below 5 °C / 41 °F) reduces battery capacity and increases the energy consumed by cabin heating — real-world range in cold climates can be 25–40 percent below the WLTP figure
- Air conditioning in hot weather has a smaller impact than heating but still reduces range by 10–20 percent

A practical planning rule: budget 70–80 percent of the WLTP figure as your realistic usable range for long-distance highway driving in typical conditions, and plan charging stops on that basis. For daily commuting in mild weather, you will often achieve close to the WLTP figure.

## Battery Capacity (kWh)

The gross battery capacity, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), is the total energy stored in the battery at 100 percent state of charge. The **usable capacity** is slightly less — typically 90–95 percent of gross — because battery management systems reserve a buffer at the top and bottom of the charge range to protect cell longevity.

A larger battery does not always deliver proportionally more range: a heavier, less aerodynamic SUV with an 80 kWh battery may deliver similar real-world range to a lighter, more aerodynamic compact car with a 70 kWh battery. The efficiency figure — miles per kWh — is the metric that tells you how far you travel on each unit of stored energy.

When comparing BEVs, look at both battery capacity and the estimated real-world efficiency simultaneously. A vehicle rated at 4 miles/kWh with a 70 kWh usable battery will typically deliver more usable real-world range than a vehicle rated at 3.5 miles/kWh with a 78 kWh usable battery, despite the larger battery in the second example.

## DC Fast-Charge Speed (kW)

The DC fast-charge rate is the maximum power the vehicle's battery management system accepts from a DC fast-charger (DCFC). This figure is measured in kilowatts (kW) and determines how quickly you can add range at a public fast-charger during a long journey.

**What the headline says**: "Accepts up to 150 kW DC fast charging."

**What it means in practice**: the stated peak figure is achieved during the early portion of the charging session when the battery is below approximately 50–60 percent state of charge. As the battery fills, the charge rate is tapered to protect the cells — a process called the charge curve. The practical metric is **time to charge from 10 to 80 percent**, which is the standard range quoted for public fast-charging stops. Many modern BEVs achieve this in 20–35 minutes at a suitable DCFC.

To use this figure, you also need to know the power output of the charger you intend to use. A 150 kW-capable vehicle connected to a 50 kW charger charges at 50 kW, not 150 kW. The bottleneck is whichever is lower — charger output or vehicle acceptance rate.

## Cargo Volume (Liters / Cubic Feet)

The cargo or trunk volume is measured in liters (European standard) or cubic feet (North American standard), and is typically provided for two configurations: seats-up (all passengers seated) and seats-folded (second row folded flat in a two-row vehicle, or third row folded in a seven-seat vehicle).

**What the headline says**: "520 liters (18.4 cu ft) with rear seats upright."

**Watch for**: the SAE standard for cargo volume measurement includes all usable space including over-sill space (the volume above the cargo sill to the roof). Some vehicles achieve higher headline figures by having a very large upper volume that is practically usable only for soft or light items. Measure the cargo floor width between the wheel arches — this is the constraint that determines whether a specific large item (a stroller, a large suitcase flat, a piece of furniture) actually fits. The headline volume figure does not tell you this.

Also note whether the vehicle has underfloor storage — a separate compartment under the cargo floor that holds the spare wheel, the charging cable, or both. This is "bonus" storage that does not affect the headline figure but adds genuine usability.

For BEVs with a front motor unit, check whether there is a **frunk** (front trunk) — additional storage under the front hood where the engine would otherwise sit. Frunk sizes vary from approximately 15 liters (0.5 cu ft, a briefcase) to over 100 liters (3.5 cu ft, meaningful luggage) depending on the platform architecture.

## Tow Rating (kg / lbs)

The tow rating is the maximum weight of a trailer the vehicle is certified to tow under the manufacturer's approved conditions. There are two figures to check:

- **Braked trailer maximum**: the maximum weight when towing a trailer equipped with its own braking system (most travel trailers, horse trailers, and boat trailers above a certain weight)
- **Unbraked trailer maximum**: the maximum weight when towing a trailer with no independent brakes (small utility trailers, bicycle carriers on a trailer platform)

**Important nuance**: the tow rating on a BEV or PHEV is sometimes lower than the equivalent ICE model at the same trim level, and towing at or near the rated maximum significantly reduces range — by 35–50 percent for a BEV towing at its maximum rated load. If towing a travel trailer is a regular requirement, confirm both the tow rating and the practical range impact before choosing an electric powertrain.

## Curb Weight (kg / lbs)

Curb weight is the weight of the vehicle as delivered — fuel in the tank (or battery at full charge), all standard fluids, but without passengers, luggage, or optional accessories. It is measured in kilograms or pounds.

Curb weight matters for three practical reasons:

1. **Payload capacity**: the difference between the gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) and the curb weight is the payload — the maximum total weight of passengers, luggage, and accessories the vehicle can carry. Overloading a vehicle affects braking performance, tire wear, and may void the manufacturer's warranty and insurance.
2. **Handling character**: heavier vehicles feel more planted at speed but take more energy to maneuver at low speed. The weight distribution — front-to-rear percentage — affects steering feel and handling balance.
3. **Running costs**: heavier vehicles use more energy per mile than lighter ones, all else being equal.

For BEVs, note that the battery pack adds 400–700 kg (880–1,540 lb) compared to an equivalent ICE model, which is why BEVs in a given segment typically have a higher curb weight than their ICE counterparts despite having fewer mechanical components.

## Putting It Together: A Practical Checklist

When evaluating a specification sheet for a shortlisted vehicle, verify:

- [ ] Real-world range estimate (70–80% of WLTP / EPA figure) covers your regular long-distance journey with one planned charge stop
- [ ] DC fast-charge speed (10–80%) is within your time tolerance for a highway stop
- [ ] Cargo volume (seats-up) accommodates your standard weekly load; seats-folded covers your largest occasional load
- [ ] Tow rating (braked) meets or exceeds your intended trailer weight plus a 10 percent margin
- [ ] Curb weight plus total passenger weight plus luggage weight stays below the GVWR
